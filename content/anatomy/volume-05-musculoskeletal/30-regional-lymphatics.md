## Breast, Thoracic Wall, Lower Limb, and Head-Neck Lymphatics

Lymphatic drainage is best memorized by pathway. Medial breast drains to parasternal nodes; lateral breast drains to pectoral axillary nodes. Medial lower limb follows great saphenous vein to superficial inguinal nodes; lateral/posterior lower limb follows small saphenous vein to popliteal nodes.

## Relationships and functional organization

Parasternal nodes drain medial breast and anterior thoracic wall. Intercostal nodes drain posterior thoracic wall and intercostal spaces. Superficial inguinal nodes drain superficial lower limb, infraumbilical abdominal wall, gluteal region, perineum, and external genitalia except testes. Deep inguinal nodes receive deep lower limb and popliteal drainage. Head/neck superficial ring drains to deep cervical nodes. Deep cervical nodes drain to jugular lymph trunk.

## Why can breast lymph cross toward parasternal or opposite-side routes?

Lymphatic networks communicate across tissue planes and midline regions. Although axillary drainage is predominant for much of the breast, medial regions have parasternal connections and additional pathways exist. The breast also has superficial and deeper networks, so a single arrow to one node group is incomplete. This anatomical variability matters when interpreting regional spread, but a teaching map does not predict an individual's exact drainage. Show major routes and communications without claiming that quadrants are sealed lymphatic compartments.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion

Lymphatic maps depend on tissue identity and development, not just present-day surface location. Testicular lymph reaches lumbar nodes while scrotal skin chiefly reaches superficial inguinal nodes. Breast drainage is predominantly axillary but includes parasternal and other routes that can cross the midline. Deep cervical nodes receive multiple head and neck pathways. Treat named drainage patterns as major routes with communications rather than perfectly sealed territories.
