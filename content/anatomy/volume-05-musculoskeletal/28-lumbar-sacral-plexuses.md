## Cervical, Lumbar, and Sacral Plexuses

The cervical plexus comes from C1-C4, the lumbar plexus mainly from L1-L4, and the sacral plexus mainly from L4-S4. Plexus knowledge lets you predict sensory and motor deficits.

## Relationships and functional organization

Cervical cutaneous branches: lesser occipital, greater auricular, transverse cervical, supraclavicular nerves. Ansa cervicalis supplies most infrahyoid muscles. Phrenic nerve is C3-C5. Lumbar plexus branches include iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, lateral femoral cutaneous, femoral, and obturator nerves. Sacral plexus branches include sciatic, tibial, common fibular, superior/inferior gluteal, and pudendal nerves.

## Why is a dermatome not identical to a peripheral nerve sensory territory?

A dermatome corresponds broadly to a spinal sensory root, while a peripheral nerve usually contains fibers from multiple roots redistributed through a plexus. Their maps therefore overlap differently. The saphenous territory along the medial leg is a named-nerve distribution, while root-level sensory maps divide the limb by segmental contributions. Both show variation and overlap. Compare sensory change with muscle actions and reflexes before assigning a level; one patch of skin alone rarely describes the entire anatomical pathway.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion

The femoral nerve arises from lumbar plexus roots L2-L4 and supplies knee extensors before its saphenous sensory continuation. Obturator L2-L4 chiefly serves thigh adductors, while sciatic L4-S3 continues into tibial and common fibular divisions. Similar roots do not mean identical peripheral territories. A root lesion can affect muscles supplied by several named nerves; a peripheral nerve lesion follows that nerve's distribution. Compare motor action, reflex and sensory pattern together.
