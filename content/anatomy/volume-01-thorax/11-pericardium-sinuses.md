## Locate the structures

The fibrous pericardium encloses the heart and roots of great vessels. Parietal serous pericardium lines its inner aspect, and visceral serous pericardium adheres to the heart as epicardium. The intervening cavity normally contains a small lubricating fluid volume. Phrenic sensory supply to fibrous and parietal pericardium helps explain referred pain.

## Connect structure and function

The transverse sinus lies posterior to the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk and anterior to the venous structures behind them. The oblique sinus is a blind recess behind the left atrium bounded by venous reflections. These are spaces created by folding of one serosal arrangement, not holes through the myocardium. Trace reflections to distinguish a continuous passage from a blind recess.

## Why can rapid fluid accumulation be more disruptive than a larger slowly accumulated volume?

The pericardial enclosure has limited short-term compliance. Rapid accumulation can raise pressure before the sac adapts, restricting chamber filling and affecting output. Slower accumulation may allow more stretching, though it can still become clinically important. The anatomical principle is pressure-volume behavior in a constrained space, not a universal volume threshold. Keep this separate from pleural effusion, which occupies a different cavity and primarily changes lung mechanics rather than directly surrounding the heart.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion
