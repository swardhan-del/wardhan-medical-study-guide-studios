## Locate the structures

The vagus carries parasympathetic and visceral sensory pathways into thoracic organs and forms esophageal plexuses. These reorganize mainly into anterior and posterior vagal trunks below, with left and right vagal contributions predominating respectively. Phrenic nerves accompany pericardiacophrenic vessels along pericardial surfaces to reach the diaphragm.

## Connect structure and function

Sympathetic trunks lie posteriorly near rib heads. Thoracic splanchnic nerves carry preganglionic sympathetic fibers onward toward abdominal ganglia, while cardiac and pulmonary sympathetic pathways have different branching destinations. A named nerve bundle may contain afferent as well as efferent fibers. Direction and target should therefore accompany the nerve name in a functional explanation.

## How do referred pain and motor weakness arise from different fiber functions?

Phrenic motor fibers activate diaphragm muscle, whereas sensory fibers return information from central diaphragmatic and pericardial regions. Shared spinal levels with somatic shoulder territories contribute to referred pain perception. A motor lesion can impair diaphragm movement without reproducing the same sensory pattern, and irritation can cause pain without complete paralysis. The nerve name alone is therefore insufficient: identify the fiber type, direction and target when explaining a clinical anatomical example.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion

Phrenic nerves pass anterior to lung roots and supply diaphragm motor function; vagus nerves pass posteriorly and contribute to pulmonary and esophageal plexuses. The left recurrent laryngeal branch loops near the ligamentum arteriosum under the aortic arch, while the right loops beneath the right subclavian artery. Thus a lesion's side and height matter. Referred shoulder pain from central diaphragmatic irritation reflects phrenic sensory levels, not a motor nerve physically ending at the shoulder.
