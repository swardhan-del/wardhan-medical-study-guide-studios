## Head and Neck Arterial Supply

The common carotid artery divides near the upper border of the thyroid cartilage into internal and external carotid arteries. The internal carotid supplies the brain and orbit; the external carotid supplies most superficial and deep structures of the face, scalp, oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, and neck.

Right common carotid comes from brachiocephalic trunk; left common carotid comes from aortic arch. External carotid branches can be remembered as: superior thyroid, ascending pharyngeal, lingual, facial, occipital, posterior auricular, maxillary, superficial temporal. Carotid sinus is a baroreceptor; carotid body is a chemoreceptor. Vertebral artery arises from subclavian artery, passes through transverse foramina, and enters skull through foramen magnum. Maxillary artery contributes to deep face, nasal cavity, teeth, palate, and dura through middle meningeal artery.

## Venous Drainage and Lymphatics of Head and Neck

Venous drainage of the head and neck converges mainly toward the internal jugular vein, external jugular vein, facial vein, pterygoid venous plexus, and dural venous sinuses. Lymph drains through superficial nodes into deep cervical nodes along the internal jugular vein.

Internal jugular vein begins as continuation of sigmoid sinus at jugular foramen. Facial vein communicates with cavernous sinus through ophthalmic venous connections and pterygoid plexus pathways. Pterygoid venous plexus communicates with facial vein and cavernous sinus. Superficial nodes include occipital, mastoid, parotid, submandibular, and submental nodes. Deep cervical nodes receive lymph from most head and neck regions and drain toward jugular lymphatic trunks.

## Why distinguish carotid sinus from carotid body when describing afferents?

The sinus is a dilation associated with stretch-sensitive baroreceptors, whereas the body is a small chemoreceptor organ near the bifurcation. Their signals concern different physiological variables even though both have important glossopharyngeal afferent connections. The internal jugular vein and vagus lie nearby in the carotid sheath, but proximity does not make vagus the only sensory pathway at the bifurcation. Describe receptor type, location and afferent route separately to avoid confusing vascular anatomy with reflex function.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion

The internal carotid supplies intracranial and orbital territories after ascending through the neck without typical cervical branches. The external carotid distributes branches to face, scalp and deep head structures. The carotid sinus senses stretch; the carotid body senses blood chemistry. Venous communications connect superficial facial and deep intracranial pathways, while lymph reaches regional nodes and the deep cervical chain. An arterial territory is therefore not a reliable substitute for a venous or lymphatic map.
