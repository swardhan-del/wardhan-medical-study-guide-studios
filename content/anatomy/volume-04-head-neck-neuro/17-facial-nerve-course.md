## CN VII Facial Nerve

The facial nerve supplies muscles of facial expression and carries taste from anterior two thirds of tongue plus parasympathetic fibers to lacrimal, submandibular, and sublingual glands.

## Relationships and functional organization

Brainstem exit: cerebellopontine angle. Skull course: internal acoustic meatus, facial canal, stylomastoid foramen. Branches in temporal bone: greater petrosal nerve, nerve to stapedius, chorda tympani. Extracranial parotid plexus gives temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, and cervical branches. Taste fibers from anterior two thirds of tongue travel with chorda tympani to geniculate ganglion and solitary nucleus.

## Why do central and peripheral facial weakness patterns differ?

Corticobulbar control of upper facial motor regions is often more bilateral than control of lower facial regions. A unilateral supranuclear lesion therefore commonly affects contralateral lower facial movement more than forehead movement. A peripheral facial nerve lesion can affect upper and lower muscles on its own side. These are useful patterns rather than absolute diagnostic rules. Branch-level lesions can be even more restricted, so anatomy should be localized from the distribution of weakness and associated taste, hearing or glandular findings.

## Apply the anatomy and resolve a common confusion

The facial nerve gives off branches at different points in its course. A proximal lesion can affect lacrimation, stapedius function or taste as well as facial movement, depending on its level. A lesion distal to the stylomastoid foramen mainly affects motor targets supplied after that exit. The named parotid branches distribute to facial expression muscles; they are not parotid secretomotor fibers. Use spared functions to localize a lesion along the route.
