# Confidence intervals explainer

The video extends the existing standard deviation and standard error lesson into confidence intervals. All numbers are fictional. The site’s existing video player provides English captions, a seekable transcript and a link back to the lesson.

## Source and review

- [NIST, Confidence Limits for the Mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) gives the one-sample t interval and explains repeated-sample coverage.
- [OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §8.1](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/8-1-a-single-population-mean-using-the-normal-distribution) provides examples of a symmetric interval, its margin of error and how sample size affects precision under the model.
- [Statistics Canada, Confidence Intervals Part 1](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/events-training/training/data-literacy/catalogue/892000062026002) is linked as optional further viewing on the publisher’s site. Its video, transcript and graphics are hosted by the publisher.

The explanation, chart and worked example are original to this site. Narration uses a synthetic macOS voice. The worked interval is 18 ± 2 minutes, which gives 16 to 20 minutes. The schematic keeps the fictional population mean fixed at 18.5 minutes. The three illustrated intervals are 16–20, 17.5–21.5 and 13.5–17.5 minutes. Two contain 18.5 and one misses it. The three draws illustrate the idea of coverage and have no statistical calibration of their own. Independent specialist review remains outstanding.

## Google Gemini Notebook production brief

Use only the two public references above and the original worked example. Choose Explainer, English, and the Whiteboard visual style. Use a clearly labelled fictional lab waiting-time dataset. Begin by showing an estimate of 18 minutes and a margin of error of 2 minutes. Draw the resulting interval from 16 to 20 minutes. Explain that a 95% confidence interval is produced by a method designed to cover a fixed population parameter about 95% of the time under its assumptions. Show separate intervals from separate samples over the same fixed reference line. Give one plausible miss and label the diagram illustrative. Leave time for the learner to decide whether 13.5–17.5 covers 18.5 minutes. Discuss why more independent observations can improve precision under the same method and why a biased sample can still mislead. Use readable units, explicit labels and no medical advice.
